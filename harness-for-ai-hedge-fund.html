The harness — how to build the machine around the model

You have the idea (an LLM that builds the economic-link graph and trades slow diffusion across it) written up in the study notes. This is the companion: the software system that turns that idea into a testable, trustworthy signal — component by component, defined before it is used, with every choice labelled build-this or skip-this and every borrowed term explained in plain English first. The recurring lesson of the whole project: the moat is the harness, not the model.

Start here · what "the harness" means

The harness is the machine around the model

Every time these notes said "the real cost is the harness," this is the thing they meant. Let's define it once, precisely, before building any of it.

▸ Definition — the harness

In software, a test harness is the scaffolding that runs a piece of code under controlled conditions, feeds it inputs, and checks its outputs — so you can trust the result. Our harness is the same idea scaled to a research strategy: it is everything except the LLM's cleverness. The pipes that carry data in, the graph the model reasons over, the code that propagates a shock, the layer that scores the answer, and — most of all — the battery of tests that decide whether the answer is real or an artefact of leakage and luck. The model is the engine; the harness is the car, the road, the crash-test rig, and the referee.

▸ In plain English

Imagine you have a brilliant analyst (the LLM) who can reason about how a tariff on Company A ripples out to A's suppliers and their suppliers. On its own, that's just an opinion. The harness is what makes the opinion bankable: it makes sure the analyst never accidentally saw tomorrow's newspaper, writes the opinion down at a fixed moment in time, turns it into a number, and then — coldly, mechanically — checks whether that number would actually have made money out of sample, after costs, versus far simpler ideas. Without the harness you cannot tell a genuine edge from a story that sounds good in hindsight.

◆ The one line to remember

Trust before signal. You build the parts that let you disbelieve your own results (identity, point-in-time data, the leakage gauntlet) before the parts that generate results (the graph, the propagation, the score). A signal you cannot trust is worse than no signal — it will get you to bet real money on noise. This ordering is the single most important design decision in the whole project, and it is why "the moat is discipline, not the model."

§Why the model is the easy 10%

Any competent fund can now prompt a strong LLM to read a filing and reason about supply chains — that is becoming table stakes. What almost nobody does well is the unglamorous 90%: resolving that "AAPL" in a 2019 filing is the same entity as today's Apple, freezing the information set so the model can't cheat, and proving after the fact that the signal isn't just the customer-momentum factor wearing a costume. That 90% is the harness, and it is where a small, disciplined team can actually be better than a big one.

▸ The failure this prevents

The classic way a research project like this dies: you wire up the exciting parts first (LLM + graph + a backtest), see a gorgeous Sharpe, get excited, and only much later discover the backtest quietly used restated data, or the LLM's training cutoff was after your test period, or the "signal" was 95% correlated with a factor you could have bought for free. Every one of those is a harness bug, not a model bug. Build the harness first and those bugs surface in week 3, not after you've raised money on a fiction.

Component 1 · the foundation

The entity spine — one identity per company, across all of time

The least glamorous component, and the one that quietly wrecks more strategies than any model bug. Nothing above it can be trusted if this is wrong.

▸ Definition — the entity spine

An entity spine (also called an identity master or security master) is a mapping that gives every company one stable internal ID, and records every external identifier it ever had — ticker, exchange symbol, CIK, LEI, CUSIP/ISIN, FIGI — and the date ranges over which each was valid. It is the dictionary that lets the rest of the harness say "this filing, this price, this news item, and this graph node are all the same company" even when the surface labels disagree.

▸ In plain English — why this is hard

Company names and tickers are not stable, and this breaks intuition badly:

  • ✕Tickers get recycled. When a company dies, its ticker is freed and later handed to a totally different company. If you join on ticker, you can silently glue Company X's 2011 prices onto Company Y's 2020 prices — an instant, invisible data-poisoning bug.
  • ✕Companies rename and re-list. Google→Alphabet, Facebook→Meta. Same economic entity, new symbol. Join on symbol and you split one company into two half-histories.
  • ✕Mergers and spin-offs. Two entities become one, or one becomes three. Who inherits the history? You must decide and record it.
  • ✕Every data vendor uses a different key. SEC uses CIK, the legal world uses LEI, Bloomberg uses FIGI, prices come keyed by ticker. Nothing lines up out of the box.

You do not need a paid security master to start. A public-domain spine that is good enough for the POC:

▸ Good vs bad — the test that proves the spine works

Good: asking "who is ticker FB on date D?" returns the right entity for every D, including after the symbol was reassigned. Bad: any join anywhere in your code that uses a raw ticker or name as a key. Write one deliberately nasty test — a recycled ticker — and make the spine pass it before you build anything else. If it can't, every downstream number is suspect.

Component 2 · the core data structure

The point-in-time event panel — freezing what was knowable

This is the single most important table in the harness. Get it right and leakage becomes hard; get it wrong and every result is a mirage.

▸ Definition — the event panel

The event panel is the central table where each row is one (event, company, timestamp): "on 2019-03-04, Company A reported an earnings surprise." Around each event the harness stores everything it needs — the graph as it stood then, the state of the neighbours, the evidence the LLM read, its estimated response, its confidence, the consensus estimate, and later the realized outcome. It is the unit of observation for the whole study: you count events, not calendar days.

▸ Definition — point-in-time (PIT) & bitemporal data

Point-in-time means every fact carries the date it became knowable, and you may only ever read facts knowable on or before your decision date. The clean way to guarantee this is a bitemporal store: each fact has two time axes — valid time (the period the fact is about, e.g. "Q4 2018 revenue") and knowledge time (when you first learned it, and when a correction superseded it). You query "as of knowledge-time D" and the store hands you exactly the world as it looked on D.

▸ In plain English — the two ways the past lies to you

1. Reporting lag. A company's Q4 revenue is about December, but you didn't learn it until the filing dropped in late February. If your backtest uses December's number in December, you traded on information from the future. 2. Restatement / vintage. Macro series and even company financials get revised. The GDP print you can pull today for 2019 is not the number that existed in 2019 — it's been cleaned up several times since. Testing on the revised number is cheating with a time machine. This is the ALFRED vintage trap: FRED gives you today's revised series; ALFRED gives you the messy number that actually existed on each past date. You must use ALFRED-style vintages.

▸ Worked example — the same fact, two timelines

Fact: "Acme Q4-2018 EPS = $1.20."

  • ·Valid time: Oct–Dec 2018 (the quarter it describes).
  • ·Knowledge time: first known 2019-02-21 08:00 (press release).
  • ·Then restated: on 2019-08-09 an amended filing changes it to $1.14.

A backtest making a decision on 2019-03-01 must see $1.20 (that's what existed then), not the corrected $1.14 — even though $1.14 is "more accurate." Accuracy in hindsight is exactly the trap. The bitemporal store returns $1.20 for any query with knowledge-time between Feb 21 and Aug 9, and $1.14 only afterwards.

2.1Purge and embargo — stopping leakage across the seam

▸ Definition — purge & embargo (López de Prado)

When you split data into train and test, a label at time t often depends on a window of future returns (your horizon is 4–8 weeks). If a training example's label window overlaps the test period, information bleeds across the seam. Purging drops training examples whose outcome windows overlap the test set; embargoing additionally removes a buffer of examples right after the test period, because nearby observations are serially correlated. Both come from Marcos López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning.

▸ In plain English

Your prediction "watches" the next 8 weeks to see if it was right. So a prediction made 3 weeks before the test window starts is still resolving when the test begins — its answer is entangled with test-period prices. Purge = "don't train on any example still watching the future when the test starts." Embargo = "and leave a gap, because last week's tremor and this week's tremor are the same earthquake." Skip this and a 4–8-week horizon leaks by construction.

▸ Good vs bad — the panel done right

Good: every column has an as-of date; the store is append-only (corrections are new rows, never overwrites); a single function world_as_of(D) returns the frozen information set; train/test are split by time with purge+embargo. Bad: a "latest value" table you UPDATE in place (you just destroyed the past), joining prices on calendar date without the reporting lag, or a random train/test split (guarantees leakage in time series). If you can't answer "what did we know at 9am on this date?" with one call, the panel isn't done.

Component 3 · where the LLM earns its keep

Graph construction — the model builds and types the network

This is the one component where the LLM does the load-bearing work. Everything the notes argued for lives here: the edge is building the graph, not reading sentiment off it.

▸ Definition — the economic-link graph

A directed, typed graph. Nodes are companies (your entity-spine IDs). Edges are economic relationships with a type and a direction: A supplies B, B is-customer-of A, A competes-with C, A and D share-input X, A and E co-located-in region R. The typing is the whole point — "the neighbour's stock moved" throws away why; a typed edge lets you reason about the mechanism.

▸ In plain English — why the LLM, and why here specifically

Disclosed supply-chain data is thin: firms only name customers above ~10% of revenue, and only the first hop. The interesting links — B's supplier depends on a component from A's region; a tariff on A raises C's input cost two hops away — are buried in the text of filings, transcripts and trade press, and they're non-obvious and cross-industry. Reading unstructured text and proposing typed, directional relationships is exactly what a strong LLM is good at and what tables miss. That is the edge. Contrast: asking the LLM "will the stock go up?" is the commoditised, decaying use everyone else does.

3.1The build, in layers

  • 1Seed with disclosed facts. Pull the hard, structured links first: ≥10% customer disclosures from EDGAR, obvious sector/competitor sets. This gives a skeleton you didn't hallucinate.
  • 2LLM proposes typed edges. Feed the model pre-event text and ask for structured output: {from, to, type, direction, sign, evidence_span, confidence}. Force it to cite the sentence it used (the evidence span) so every edge is auditable.
  • 3Constrain, don't trust. Reject edges with no evidence span, dedupe against the spine, cap out-degree, and keep the confidence for later weighting. The LLM supplies structure and sign; it does not get to assert magnitudes.
▸ The leakage rule that governs this whole component

The LLM must build the graph for event at time t using only knowledge that existed before t. Two independent guards, because one is never enough:

  • 1Input-side: feed it only documents from the PIT panel with knowledge-time < t. Never today's Wikipedia, never a filing that post-dates the event.
  • 2Weights-side: use an open-weight model with a documented training cutoff earlier than your test period. A closed API model may have read the future during pre-training and you can never prove it didn't. This is why the notes insist on open weights here — not for pruning, for auditable cutoff.
▸ Definition — GraphRAG (the retrieval pattern, optional)

GraphRAG (Microsoft, 2024) is retrieval-augmented generation where the model is grounded on a graph of extracted entities and relations rather than a flat pile of text — so it can answer multi-hop questions ("what depends on what depends on A?") coherently. It's a useful pattern for querying the graph once built; it is not required to build it, and adds machinery, so treat it as a later nice-to-have, not a v1 need.

▸ Good vs bad — a graph you can defend

Good: every edge carries a type, a direction, a cited evidence span, a confidence, and a knowledge-time; the whole graph can be regenerated deterministically from a pinned model + pinned inputs. Bad: an untyped "these two are related" blob, edges with no citation (you can't audit a hallucination), or a graph built once from today's data and reused for historical events (massive look-ahead). A defensible graph is one where, for any edge, you can point to the sentence and the date.

Component 4 · the deterministic core

The propagation engine — spreading a shock across the graph

Once the graph exists, a shock at one company has to ripple to its neighbours and their neighbours. This is plain, deterministic code — and it must stay that way.

▸ Definition — message passing

Message passing is the standard way computation happens on a graph (it's the engine inside every Graph Neural Network, and inside AgentTorch). Three steps, repeated once per hop: each edge computes a message from source to destination; each node aggregates the messages arriving at it (sum, mean, max); each node updates its state from the aggregate. Run it once = effects travel one hop. Run it k times = effects travel k hops.

▸ In plain English

Drop a stone (the shock) in a pond at node A. Round 1: every company directly linked to A feels a ripple whose size depends on the link type and strength. Round 2: those companies pass a (weaker) ripple to their neighbours. After a few rounds the ripple has spread across the map and faded out. "How far and how strong" is set by the edge weights and a decay factor — not by the LLM, which never touches this loop.

▸ Worked example — one hop

Shock: A's demand drops 10% (−0.10 at node A). Edges: A←supplies B (elasticity 0.6), A competes-with C (sign flips, 0.3).

  • ·Message A→B: −0.10 × 0.6 = −0.06 to B's demand (its customer bought less).
  • ·Message A→C: −0.10 × 0.3 × (−1) = +0.03 to C (a competitor's weakness helps C).

B and C update their state; next hop, B passes its −0.06 (decayed) to B's suppliers. The sign on each edge came from the mechanism classifier (§5); the magnitude (elasticity) is a parameter you calibrate from data — never a number the LLM made up.

4.1The design rule: keep the LLM out of the loop

▸ The division of labour (the AgentTorch lesson)

The LLM supplies direction and structure (which edges exist, what type, what sign). Deterministic code supplies magnitude and dynamics (the elasticities, the decay, the arithmetic). This split is the whole reason the system is trustworthy: deterministic code doesn't drift. The same shock through the same graph gives the same numbers every time — you can debug it, unit-test it, and calibrate it. If you let the LLM do the arithmetic, you get a different answer every run and nothing to test. (In AgentTorch this shows up literally: a float() cast severs the LLM output from the gradient — the model proposes, the optimiser disposes.)

▸ Optional: make it differentiable (calibration for free)

If you write the propagation in a tensor framework (PyTorch), the elasticities and decay become learnable parameters. Then calibration — fitting them so the model's predicted fundamentals match realized ones — is one gradient-descent loop, and sensitivity analysis ("which parameter moves the answer most?") is just reading the gradient ∂outcome/∂param from a single backward pass, instead of re-running the sim N times. This is the genuinely reusable 20% of the AgentTorch idea. It's a v1.5 optimisation, not a v1 requirement — a plain NumPy loop is fine to start.

4.2The failure mode this component owns: propagation explosion

Ripples don't just fade — the number of nodes touched grows geometrically with each hop. Push too far and "everything affects everything," the signal drowns in noise, and later hops are pure hallucination. You must cap hops and decay hard. Move the sliders to feel it:

Branching = how many neighbours an average node has. "Signal retained" is how much of the effect survives each hop (the rest is decay + noise).

▸ Good vs bad — reading the calculator

Good: few hops (2–3), meaningful decay, so a strong per-node effect reaches a small, nameable set of companies you can actually inspect. Bad: 5–6 hops with weak decay — thousands of nodes each nudged by a rounding error. When the effect-per-node falls below the noise floor, you're not simulating an economy, you're generating confetti. The calculator makes the tradeoff visible: every extra hop multiplies reach but shrinks effect. Stop while each touched node still has a defensible story.

Component 5 · from "it moved" to "here's why and by how much"

Mechanism & response functions

The propagation engine needs to know, for each shock: what kind is it, which direction it pushes a neighbour's fundamentals, and — eventually — how much. That's this component.

▸ Definition — the mechanism classifier

A mechanism classifier takes a shock and labels it: demand shock, cost/input shock, capacity shock, financing shock, regulatory shock, substitution shock… — and, per outgoing edge type, the sign it implies for the neighbour's revenue or margin. Example: "major customer cuts orders" is a demand shock; along a supplies→ edge its sign on the supplier's revenue is negative; along a competes-with edge to a rival supplier it may be positive.

▸ In plain English — why the sign matters more than the move

A customer's stock jumping +20% is ambiguous — it could mean "ordering more" (good for suppliers) or "cut costs / laid off staff / did a buyback" (neutral-to-bad for suppliers). The customer-momentum signal that every fund already runs collapses all of that into one number and hopes. Recovering the mechanism — was it demand or cost-cutting? — is the incremental information the whole thesis is betting on. If you can't articulate the mechanism, you have nothing the simple signal doesn't already have.

▸ Definition — response function

A response function is the numeric part: given a shock of a certain type and size arriving at a company, how much does this company's revenue/margin/EPS move? It's the elasticity the propagation engine multiplies by. Two firms react differently to the same shock (one has pricing power, one doesn't), so response functions are estimated per firm — but most firms have too little history to estimate alone.

5.1Cross-sectional shrinkage — borrowing strength across firms

▸ Definition — shrinkage (partial pooling)

Shrinkage pulls a noisy per-firm estimate toward a more stable group prior:

eq. 5.1 estimatei = λ · (company i's own history) + (1 − λ) · (peer/industry prior) λ (0–1) rises with how much reliable history firm i has. Data-rich firm → λ near 1 (trust its own numbers). Sparse firm → λ near 0 (lean on peers).
▸ In plain English — and why it beats a model-per-company

If you fit a separate model for each company, the small/new firms — often exactly where the mispricing lives — get wild estimates from three data points. Shrinkage says: "I've barely seen this firm, so assume it behaves like its industry until it proves otherwise." As evidence accumulates, λ rises and the estimate earns its independence. It's the statistically honest way to have an opinion about a firm you've only seen a few times, and it's why the research plan insists on cross-sectional learning, not one model per name.

▸ Good vs bad

Good: a small, closed set of shock types with clearly signed effects per edge type; response magnitudes shrunk toward industry priors and calibrated on realized fundamentals. Bad: a free-text "impact" the LLM writes per event (unstable, unbacktestable, drifts every run), or an ever-growing taxonomy of 200 bespoke shock types — that's overfitting the event taxonomy, a named failure mode. Keep the taxonomy small enough that each type has enough events to validate.

Component 6 · turning simulation into one number

Scoring — the RippleSignal

The propagation gives you an expected change in a company's fundamentals. The score turns that into a tradable signal by comparing it to what the market already expects.

▸ Definition — the gap vs consensus

The signal is not "we think EPS will rise." It's "we think EPS will rise more than the analysts do." You take the harness's estimated ΔEPS for a name, subtract the consensus estimate (what's already priced in), and the residual — the surprise the crowd is missing — is the RippleSignal. Trading requires an edge over consensus, not a correct forecast the market already shares.

eq. 6.1 RippleSignali,t = ΔEPSi(harness) − ΔEPSi(consensus) Positive → the harness expects a fundamental surprise the market hasn't priced → candidate long. Negative → candidate short. Magnitude and confidence set position size later.

6.1The one test the whole strategy exists to pass

▸ Definition — the incremental-value regression

The signal is only worth building if it adds information beyond the cheap signal everyone has. You test that with one cross-sectional regression:

eq. 6.2 ri,t+1 = α + β1·CustomerMomentumi,t + β2·RippleSignali,t + controls + εi,t r = next-period return. The question is entirely about β2: is it significant, stable, and the right sign after customer momentum and standard factors are already in the regression?
▸ In plain English — what a win and a loss look like

Put both signals in the same race. If β2 stays positive and significant with customer momentum sitting right next to it, the harness knows something the simple signal doesn't — that's the win. If β2 collapses to ~0 once customer momentum is in the regression, then all your machinery just re-derived the free signal the long way round — and the honest move is to use the simple one. This single coefficient is the go/no-go the entire project rides on.

Component 7 · the referee · build this FIRST

The leakage gauntlet — the part that lets you disbelieve yourself

This is the heart of the harness and the reason the moat is discipline. It is a battery of tests designed to kill your signal. Whatever survives, you can trust. Build it before the signal it judges.

▸ Why it comes first

If you build the signal first, you will fall in love with it, and then every test you write will be (unconsciously) one it can pass. Build the referee while you have nothing to defend, and the first thing you run through it is a signal you know is fake — to confirm the gauntlet catches it. A gauntlet that never rejects anything is decoration. "Prove the harness catches a known-fake signal" is the acceptance test for this whole component.

7.1The tests, defined and explained

▸ Placebo / negative control

What: run the exact pipeline on an input that should produce nothing — a random graph, a shuffled event calendar, a fake company. Plain English: a pregnancy test that reads positive on tap water is broken. If your pipeline "finds alpha" in noise, the alpha it finds in real data is also noise. Pass: placebo signal ≈ 0.

▸ Label-shuffle (permutation test)

What: randomly re-pair each prediction with some other event's realized outcome, thousands of times, and rebuild the performance distribution. Plain English: if your real Sharpe sits comfortably inside the cloud of Sharpes you get from deliberately mismatched labels, your result is what randomness produces — you've measured luck. Pass: real performance in the far tail of the shuffled distribution.

▸ Anonymisation test

What: strip company names/tickers and any identifying strings from what the LLM sees, so it can't recognise "oh, this is the 2020 COVID crash" and pattern-match the outcome from memory. Plain English: you want the model reasoning from the mechanism, not recalling how the story ended. Pass: signal survives anonymisation (if it collapses, it was memory/leakage, not reasoning).

▸ Deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey & López de Prado)

What: a corrected Sharpe that penalises you for how many strategies you tried and for non-normal returns. Try 100 variants and the best one looks great by chance alone; the deflated Sharpe asks "is this better than the best you'd expect from 100 coin-flips?" Plain English: torture the data long enough and it confesses to anything — this test prices in the torture. Pass: deflated Sharpe still clears your bar. (Corollary: pre-commit to one strategy so the penalty stays near zero — a recurring theme in the notes.)

▸ PBO — probability of backtest overfitting (Bailey et al.)

What: repeatedly split your trials into two halves; how often does the config that looked best in-sample underperform out-of-sample? That frequency is the PBO. Plain English: it directly estimates "what's the chance my 'best' result is just the luckiest overfit?" Pass: PBO comfortably below 50% (ideally well under).

▸ Fama–MacBeth — count events, not rows

What: estimate the cross-sectional relationship date by date, then average the per-date coefficients and test that series. Plain English: 5,000 stocks on the same day in the same market crash are not 5,000 independent facts — they're closer to one. Fama–MacBeth stops you from mistaking a handful of market-wide events for a huge, significant sample. Pass: the averaged coefficient is significant on the count of independent periods, not the inflated row count.

▸ Cutoff & PIT audit (the leakage-specific ones)

What: automated checks that (a) the LLM's training cutoff predates every test event, and (b) every fact used by every decision has knowledge-time < decision-time. Plain English: the two ways the future sneaks in — through the model's memory and through the data pipe — each get their own tripwire. Pass: zero facts with a future knowledge-time; documented, verified model cutoff.

▸ The mindset: guilty until proven innocent

Treat every positive result as leakage until you've ruled it out. The notes are blunt about this: leakage is the default hypothesis, not the exception. The gauntlet is how you discharge the burden of proof. A signal that survives placebo + shuffle + anonymisation + deflated-Sharpe + PBO + a clean PIT audit is one you've genuinely tried to kill and failed. That — not a pretty equity curve — is what earns real capital.

▸ Good vs bad — the gauntlet itself

Good: the gauntlet runs automatically on every result, rejects known-fakes, and its verdict is recorded next to the signal. Bad: tests you run once, by hand, only on the result you like; or a gauntlet you weaken after it rejects your favourite signal. The moment you start negotiating with the referee, you've lost the only edge a small team has here.

Component 8 · the bar to clear

The baseline ladder — what the signal must beat

"The agents gave plausible answers" is not a result. The result is: the harness beats every simpler, cheaper thing that could explain the same returns. You build those simpler things on purpose, as opponents.

▸ In plain English — why you build your own competition

Every rung of this ladder is a way your fancy signal could secretly be something boring. If a plain factor model, or a single LLM with search, or the graph with the mechanism ripped out, does just as well, then the expensive machinery earned nothing. You climb the ladder to force the honest question at each rung: "did this specific piece add anything?"

▸ Definition — ablation

An ablation removes one component and re-measures. The bottom three rows are ablations of your own harness: kill the mechanism classifier, kill multi-hop, and see if the score still works. This is how you attribute performance to a part, not to the whole black box — and how you find out which components you can throw away to make v1 simpler.

▸ Good vs bad

Good: every baseline runs through the same gauntlet, PIT panel and cost model as the real signal — an apples-to-apples race. Bad: comparing your carefully-tuned signal to a strawman baseline you ran once with defaults. If you want to believe you beat customer momentum, build the best honest version of customer momentum and beat that.

Component 9 · from signal to (paper) P&L

Backtest & portfolio — walking forward, netting costs

A signal that predicts returns is not yet a strategy. This component turns per-name scores into positions, walks them through time honestly, and subtracts the frictions that kill most paper edges.

▸ Definition — walk-forward (out-of-sample) testing

Walk-forward testing respects the arrow of time: fit on an early window, validate on the next window, and touch a single most-recent, never-seen window exactly once, at the very end, as the real test. Never a random train/test split — in time series that shuffles the future into the training set. You get one honest look at the final test period; spend it wisely.

▸ In plain English — the one-shot final test

The most-recent slice of data is a non-renewable resource. Every time you peek at it and then tweak the model, you've contaminated it — it's now part of your training, and its verdict no longer means anything. So you develop entirely on earlier data, lock everything, and run the final period once. If it fails, you don't get to "try again" on that period; you go back to theory. That discipline is what makes the final number believable.

▸ Definition — the portfolio optimiser

Alpha (what to bet on) and risk (how much) are kept separate. The optimiser chooses position weights w:

eq. 9.1 maxw   α̂Tw  −  λ·wTΣw  −  γ·Turnover(w) α̂ = predicted alpha (your RippleSignal). Σ = covariance (risk). λ = risk aversion, γ = trading-cost aversion. Solved under dollar-, beta- and sector-neutrality so you're betting on the ripple, not on the market or a sector drifting.
▸ In plain English — each term

First term: lean into names with high predicted alpha. Second: but don't pile into correlated bets that blow up together (that's the risk penalty). Third: and don't churn the book so hard that trading costs eat the edge. Neutrality constraints strip out the market/sector so that if you make money, it's from the ripple insight and not from accidentally being long tech in a tech rally.

▸ The failure this component owns: the capacity/cost gap

Many paper edges are real but tiny, and live in small, illiquid names — so real trading costs and limited capacity erase them. The backtest must charge realistic spreads, market impact and borrow costs, and report net Sharpe and how much capital the signal can actually absorb. A gross-of-cost, microcap-concentrated Sharpe (the trap several of the cited papers fall into) is not a business.

▸ Good vs bad

Good: walk-forward, one-shot final test, costs and capacity modelled, results reported net and risk-neutralised. Bad: in-sample Sharpe, gross of costs, on names you could never trade at size — the classic way a "great backtest" becomes a losing fund.

Component 10 · the plumbing that makes it repeatable

Runtime & reproducibility

The last component is the least visible: the orchestration that makes a run cheap, cached, and — above all — reproducible. A result you can't reproduce is a rumour.

▸ Definition — the archetype trick (keeping LLM calls cheap)

You do not call the LLM once per company per event — that's millions of calls. Following AgentTorch's archetype idea: group firms into a few behavioural archetypes, call the LLM once per archetype to get its response pattern, then apply that pattern deterministically to every firm in the group. Tens of calls, not millions. Compute stops being the constraint; engineering time becomes the real cost — which, as the notes stress, is exactly where the value is, so spend it there.

▸ Definition — reproducibility (the run ledger)

A run is reproducible if re-running it later gives the same result. That requires pinning four things and recording them in a run ledger: (1) the model weights + version + cutoff, (2) the exact data vintage (as-of snapshot), (3) all random seeds, (4) the code commit. Cache LLM outputs keyed by (prompt, model-hash) so re-runs are cheap and identical.

▸ In plain English — why this is a trust feature, not a nicety

Say you get a promising result in March. In June an investor asks you to reproduce it. If your LLM was a closed API that silently updated, your data was overwritten in place, and your seeds weren't fixed, you cannot — and now you don't know if March was real. Pinning everything means any past result can be regenerated bit-for-bit. It's the same discipline as the PIT panel, applied to your own experiments: freeze what you knew and how you computed it.

▸ The reassuring part: the bill

With the archetype trick, realistic POC LLM compute is hundreds to low-thousands of dollars, self-hosted or via API; public data is ~$300–700/yr. The dominant cost is your engineering time on the spine, the PIT panel and the gauntlet. That's not a bug — those are the components that are hard to copy, so time spent there is the moat being built. (The interactive cost calculator in sim-alpha.html lets you scale these numbers.)

Putting it together · the sequence

Build order — trust before signal, in weeks

The components have a run-time order (data → signal → judge) and a build order. They are not the same. You build the things that let you distrust results before the things that produce them. This is the 12-week path from the study notes, mapped onto the components above.

Wk 0data
Spine + data stack. Stand up EDGAR / GDELT / Treasury-ALFRED ingest and the identity spine (CIK/LEI/OpenFIGI). Pin an open-weight model with a documented cutoff. → §1, §10
Wk 1–2rails
The PIT event panel. (event, company, timestamp) with as-of dates on everything; prices/fundamentals wired point-in-time; purge/embargo plumbing. → §2
Wk 3–4trust

Wk 11–12decide

▸ The one thing this document is not

This is a how to build the machine guide. It does not claim the machine will find alpha — that's the job of the study notes' evidence and, ultimately, of running the gauntlet on real data. A beautifully built harness around a signal that doesn't exist is still zero. The value of the harness is precisely that it will tell you that, quickly and honestly, if it's true.
